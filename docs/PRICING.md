# Launch pricing

## The recommendation

| | Price | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Monthly** | **$59 / month** | The headline price |
| **Annual** | **$590 / year** | Two months free, works out at $49 a month |
| **Trial** | **14 days, no card** | Full access, one per person |
| **Founding member** | **$39 / month or $390 / year**, first 50 subscribers, locked while the subscription stays active | A promotion code in Stripe |

One product, one price, no tiers. Everything FIRE does is in every
subscription.

## Why $59

**Above the disposable band.** Anything at or under $29 reads as a browser
extension, and it attracts the customers who generate the most support for the
least revenue. A trader deciding whether to point real money through a tool
treats a suspiciously cheap one as a warning.

**Below the institutional band.** At $99 and up you are compared with Bookmap
and Sierra Chart, products with a decade of features and a support department.
FIRE is one focused thing done well. Pricing it there invites a comparison it
loses on breadth.

**It clears a low bar to be worth it.** At $59 a month the customer needs FIRE
to be worth about two dollars a day. One avoided fat finger, or one fill that
lands where the screen said rather than twenty cents worse, covers a month. That
is an argument a trader can make to themselves in one sentence, which is what
you want a price to do.

**It survives discounting.** $59 leaves room for the founding member price, an
annual discount and the occasional retention offer without ever going below what
the product is worth.

## Why annual at two months free

Two months free is the convention, it is trivially explainable, and it lands at
$49 which reads as a different price band without being one. It also matters
more than usual here: annual subscribers do not churn on a bad trading week,
and this product will have customers who have bad trading weeks.

Expect a minority to take it at launch. Its real job is to be the anchor that
makes $59 monthly look like the flexible option.

## Why a trial, and why with no card

Demo mode already does the top of funnel work: anybody can see the terminal
without paying or connecting anything. The trial exists for the one thing demo
cannot show, which is FIRE against a real book with real money.

No card, because requiring one on a specialised tool with a narrow audience
suppresses exactly the qualified signups you want, and because the abuse risk
is low: a trial is only useful to someone who already has a funded exchange
account and an API key. The friction that matters is already there and it is
theirs, not ours.

Fourteen days rather than seven, because these are short duration markets and
somebody needs to catch a few different sessions before they know.

## Why a founding member price

Three reasons, in order of importance.

**You need people who will tell you what is broken.** Fifty customers who feel
they got in early will write to you when something is wrong. Fifty customers who
paid full price will just cancel.

**It creates a reason to buy now.** A discount that expires by count rather than
by date is credible and it does not need a fake countdown clock.

**It caps what you give away.** Fifty seats at twenty dollars off is a thousand
dollars a month at full take up, and it buys the testimonials and the bug
reports that the next hundred customers depend on.

Lock the price for as long as the subscription stays active, and say so plainly.
A discount that quietly reverts after a year produces a wave of cancellations
in month thirteen and a reputation you do not want.

## What not to do at launch

**No usage pricing.** Charging per order aligns your revenue with the customer
trading more, which is a bad thing to want and a worse thing to be seen to want
by anybody looking at this from a regulatory angle.

**No lifetime deal.** It is a loan against a product that has recurring costs,
and it prices in an assumption about how long you will support this that you
cannot make yet.

**No free tier beyond demo.** Demo already is the free tier. A free live tier
means supporting people who will never pay while they route real money through
your software.

**No tiers yet.** Splitting features between a cheap plan and an expensive one
before you know which features people actually value means guessing, and every
guess becomes a migration later.

## When to revisit

Raise the price when you have thirty paying customers who renewed at least once
and the support load per customer is falling. Grandfather everyone who is
already paying. Nothing costs goodwill faster than an existing customer finding
out their price went up while a new one got a better deal.
